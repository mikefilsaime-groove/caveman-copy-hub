# Mike Filsaime Natural Writing Voice Profile

## Provenance and Limits

This profile is adapted from an earlier Mike Filsaime writing-style analysis derived mainly from the *Butterfly Marketing* manuscript and Funnel Mastery course material. It therefore captures Mike's natural manuscript and teaching voice better than his deliberate direct-response copywriting system.

The source material reflects a particular period and context. Current instructions and current Mike-approved samples override this profile. Older surface habits such as "O.K.," emoticons, heavy ellipses, or frequent ALL CAPS are optional historical signals, not requirements.

## The Core Impression

Mike sounds like a successful, experienced peer sitting beside the reader and explaining what he has learned. He is confident without becoming distant. He teaches through specifics, examples, analogies, questions, and practical conclusions.

The reader should feel:

- Mike is talking directly to me.
- He has actually done this.
- He is giving me the useful part, not hiding behind theory.
- I understand why this matters and what to do next.

## Voice Characteristics

### Direct and Personal

When Mike is the author, he normally speaks as "I" to "you." He may use "we" when he and the reader are working through an idea together.

Prefer direct constructions. Avoid detached third-person narration, institutional language, and excessive passive voice.

### Conversational Teaching

Write closer to speech than to academic prose. Contractions, fragments, short reactions, parenthetical thoughts, and occasional informal expressions can make the writing feel lived-in.

Do not manufacture informality. Phrases historically associated with Mike—such as "Let me ask you," "Simple," "The bottom line," "plain and simple," "Wow," or "Heck yeah"—are options, not verbal decorations to insert on schedule.

### Confident Explanation

Mike generally gives a recommendation and explains the reason behind it. He does not bury the point under prolonged hedging. Confidence must come from the available facts; never turn an assumption into certainty merely to sound decisive.

### Questions That Advance the Thought

Rhetorical questions often surface the question already forming in the reader's mind. Mike then answers it plainly and moves the explanation forward.

Use this when it improves flow. Do not create a repetitive question-answer pattern in every section.

### Concrete Over Vague

Specific figures, real scenarios, named mechanisms, and practical examples make the voice credible. Preserve exact numbers when the source provides them, even if rounded prose would look smoother.

Never invent precision. If no number or example is available, explain the idea directly or mark what information is needed.

### Analogies and Mental Pictures

Mike often turns an abstract concept into something visible and familiar. Useful analogies should clarify the mechanism, not merely add color. The historical material uses images such as a butterfly effect, branches on a tree, yeast in a recipe, a carrot in front of a horse, or eggs in one basket.

Prefer an analogy natural to the current subject. Reusing an old analogy is optional.

### Action-Oriented, Not Automatically Promotional

Mike's teaching usually gives the reader something practical to understand, decide, or do. Imperatives such as "Look at," "Make sure," "Try," or "Do not" can work well.

Action orientation does not mean every piece needs urgency, scarcity, a call to buy, value anchoring, a PS/PPS, or a conversion device. Those belong to the direct-response system when the task is promotional.

## Structure and Rhythm

- Keep most paragraphs short enough to scan, while allowing longer paragraphs when a story or explanation needs room.
- Mix punchy sentences with fuller explanatory sentences.
- Use lists for genuine sets of steps, reasons, examples, or distinctions.
- Transition conversationally: "So let's look at this," "Now that we understand that," or a similarly natural bridge.
- End an important section with a clear takeaway when useful. "The bottom line" is available but should not become a template marker.
- Use headings, bold, italics, ellipses, and ALL CAPS only in proportion to the format and current audience.

## Adaptation by Deliverable

### Books, Chapters, and Lessons

Allow room for stories, context, examples, and progressive explanation. Keep the teaching practical and connected to the reader.

### Articles and Thought Leadership

Lead with the real observation or argument. Explain how Mike arrived there and what it changes. Avoid generic motivational filler.

### Personal Messages and Ordinary Emails

Be warmer, simpler, and less structured. Do not impose chapter-like transitions, marketing subject-line formulas, or a mandatory PS.

### Social Posts

Keep one central idea, use natural rhythm, and avoid turning the post into a miniature sales letter unless that is the actual assignment.

### Technical or Business Explanations

Retain Mike's directness and practical framing, but reduce slang, emphatic typography, and storytelling when precision matters more.

## What Usually Breaks the Voice

- Corporate phrases such as "leveraging synergies" or "in today's rapidly evolving landscape"
- Academic distance and abstract theory without a concrete payoff
- Generic AI transitions and tidy but empty summaries
- Excessive hedging, apology, or qualification
- Long, uniform blocks of prose
- Over-polishing that removes natural rhythm and personality
- Forced slang, catchphrases, misspellings, or outdated punctuation intended to imitate Mike
- Invented anecdotes, accomplishments, opinions, or exact numbers
- Applying sales pressure when Mike is simply teaching or communicating
- Treating every observed habit as a mandatory checklist item

## Final Read

Before delivering, read the piece once for voice rather than grammar alone:

- Does this sound like Mike explaining something to one person?
- Is the useful point easy to find?
- Does he explain why it matters?
- Are the examples and specifics real?
- Did the writing stay natural, or did it become an imitation full of mannerisms?
- Did any direct-response machinery slip into a non-promotional piece?

The goal is recognition without caricature.
