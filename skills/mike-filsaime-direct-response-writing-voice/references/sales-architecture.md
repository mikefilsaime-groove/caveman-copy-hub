# Mike Filsaime Direct Response Sales Architecture

This is a flexible architecture distilled from Mike's authored sales copy. It is not a mandatory template. Use only the sections needed for the audience, offer, awareness level, and format.

## 1. Lead With Tension, Not Background

Open on a result, frustration, contradiction, warning, or opportunity with enough specificity to earn attention. Mike's strongest leads imply that the reader's current model is incomplete.

The opening should answer at least one of these questions:

- What does the reader desperately want?
- What are they tired of?
- What surprising result demands explanation?
- What common behavior is keeping them stuck?
- What new mechanism changes the calculation?

Do not begin with company history unless the history itself creates the tension.

## 2. Join the Reader Against the Problem

Name the frustration in the reader's own practical world. Mike often identifies a shared enemy: hype, wasted purchases, technical complexity, expensive programmers, dependence on other marketers, or repeated effort without an owned asset.

Make clear that Mike has experienced or observed the same problem. This earns identification before authority.

## 3. Move From Ordinary Person to Credible Guide

Use only relevant pieces of Mike's story. A useful story establishes:

- Where he started
- What he believed or struggled with
- The decision, discovery, or constraint that changed the path
- What happened after applying the mechanism
- Why that experience helps the reader now

Do not paste a standard biography into every promotion. The story exists to make the mechanism and promise believable.

## 4. Build a Proof Ladder

Mike layers proof rather than relying on one assertion. Depending on what is verified and available, move through:

1. A precise personal result or demonstration
2. Screenshots, records, video, product behavior, or observable evidence
3. Multiple examples across products, sites, or periods
4. Customer outcomes or expert endorsements supplied by the user
5. A reasonable explanation of why the result occurred

Testimonials are proof assets, not source material for Mike's voice. Preserve their original wording and attribution when provided; never manufacture or silently rewrite them.

## 5. Reveal and Teach the Mechanism

Name the core idea and make it understandable. A useful mechanism explains:

- Why the old approach underperforms
- What changes in the new approach
- Why a small action can produce a larger result
- How the product makes implementation easier, faster, safer, or more reliable

Teach enough to create belief. Do not hide an empty offer behind "secret" language.

## 6. Show the Old Way and New Way

Make the operational contrast visible. Describe the cost, delay, confusion, dependence, or risk in the old path. Then show the steps, leverage, ownership, or simplicity in the new path.

The contrast should naturally position the offer as the bridge.

## 7. Translate Features Through Three Levels

For important components, move through:

1. **What it is**
2. **What it enables operationally**
3. **What that changes for the buyer**

For example, software is not valuable merely because it contains modules. It may let the buyer launch without waiting months for a programmer, retain control, and test more ideas with less risk.

## 8. Segment When It Increases Recognition

Mike sometimes shows how the offer serves people at different experience levels. Use audience segments only when the product truly supports distinct use cases. Give each segment a recognizable outcome rather than changing only the label.

## 9. Handle Objections as a Conversation

Do not dump objections into a generic FAQ. Introduce them when the reader would naturally feel them.

A strong Mike-style objection sequence is:

1. Voice the objection plainly.
2. Confirm why it is reasonable.
3. Reframe it through proof, economics, mechanism, or risk.
4. State the practical conclusion.
5. Move directly back toward the decision.

For a long promotion, a numbered "reasons you may not have acted" structure can work when the reasons are real and complete.

## 10. Present the Offer as Implementation

The offer should feel like the logical way to apply what the prospect now believes. Organize it around the job each component performs.

- Lead with the core vehicle.
- Explain the function of each major component.
- Add bonuses that remove a barrier, accelerate use, or deepen the result.
- Avoid stacking unrelated files solely to inflate value.
- Use verified price comparisons and values only.

## 11. Explain the Price and Buying Logic

Mike often reasons through price rather than simply displaying it. Useful approaches include comparing price with the cost of the old path, the value of the result, the time saved, or the verified value of components.

If there is an introductory price, payment plan, future increase, or unusual pricing decision, explain the real reason. Transparency is part of the voice.

## 12. Reverse Risk and Qualify Responsibility

State what the guarantee covers, for how long, what the buyer must do, and how a refund works. Plain language builds more trust than decorative "iron-clad" labels alone.

Mike may pair risk reversal with buyer qualification: the product can provide the method, but the buyer must read, implement, or take action. Keep this fair and consistent with the actual terms.

## 13. Give a Truthful Reason to Act Now

Urgency may come from a real deadline, capacity limit, inventory, bonus window, price change, event date, or strategic launch period. Explain the reason.

If no real urgency exists, do not invent it. Use the cost of delay or the value of starting now without pretending the offer disappears.

## 14. Make the Order Decision Concrete

Restate what the buyer receives, the price, guarantee, key terms, and immediate next step. Mike's order language often lets the reader mentally say yes to specific outcomes.

Keep the CTA unmistakable. Do not make the reader hunt for what to do.

## 15. Close From Multiple Angles

A full close may combine:

- Rational recap
- Identity and responsibility
- Future pacing
- Risk reversal
- Cost of staying in the same place
- Real urgency
- Direct instruction

Postscripts can carry distinct closing arguments. Each PS should add a new reason or resolve a final hesitation; do not repeat the same sentence with more punctuation.

## Format Adaptation

### Promotional Email

Use one dominant tension, one proof element, one mechanism insight, and one action. Do not compress an entire sales letter into every email. Across a sequence, distribute story, proof, teaching, objections, offer, and urgency.

### VSL or Webinar Pitch

Preserve the conversational question-answer rhythm. Use visual proof where spoken claims would otherwise feel abstract. Transition from teaching to offer by showing that the product implements the mechanism already taught.

### Short Landing Page or Ad

Keep the core contrast and specificity, but remove long backstory and repeated closes. Let the next step carry the deeper argument.

### Long-Form Sales Letter

Use the full emotional and logical progression, but update typography and pacing for the current reading environment. Repetition should deepen belief or answer a new objection, not merely lengthen the page.

## Final Sales Check

- One central promise is clear.
- A believable mechanism supports it.
- Proof is verified and interpreted.
- The offer implements the mechanism.
- The real objections are answered.
- Price, guarantee, terms, and urgency are accurate.
- The prospect knows exactly what to do.
- The copy can persuade without testimonials doing all the work.
- Removing ALL CAPS and exclamation points would not collapse the argument.
