# Mike Filsaime Promotional Email Method

## Purpose and Evidence

Use this guidance for conversion-oriented emails and email campaigns. It is derived from Mike Filsaime's two *Email Copy Clinic* training transcripts, in which he explains how he evaluates, rewrites, sequences, and improves promotional email.

The trainings are stronger evidence of Mike's email-marketing judgment than of uniquely Mike-authored surface phrasing. Some discussed emails were written or co-written by Andy Jenkins, Jason Fladlien, or affiliate partners before Mike edited them. Apply the principles confidently; treat playful phrases, punctuation habits, and isolated wording as optional unless the current task supplies Mike-authored email samples.

## Start With One Person

Write as one person speaking to one person. Picture a specific reader and continue a real conversation with that person.

- Prefer `I` and `you` to broadcast language such as "Hey guys."
- Make the email feel personally written rather than like corporate HTML.
- For affiliate copy, extract the truthful facts and offer details, then rewrite from Mike's point of view. Do not lightly personalize a vendor swipe and call it Mike's voice.
- Never use fake intimacy such as claiming Mike immediately thought of the reader unless that is genuinely true.

## Give Every Email a Campaign Job

Treat a campaign as a conversation across time, not a stack of interchangeable broadcasts. Identify the reader's current awareness and the next belief or action the email must produce.

A common progression is:

1. **Curiosity and relevance:** interrupt the pattern and open a loop without triggering premature objections.
2. **Story and problem:** make the issue personal, recognizable, and worth solving.
3. **Mechanism and discovery:** explain what is new, different, or newly understood.
4. **Proof and objections:** demonstrate the claim, interpret the evidence, and dispel misconceptions.
5. **Explicit invitation:** clearly present the event, product, or offer and its core benefit.
6. **Decision and deadline:** use increasingly direct reminder or "Tick Tock" emails when a real deadline approaches.

Do not force all six stages into every campaign. Preserve continuity: each email should feel like the next turn in the conversation.

## Move From Blind to Explicit

Early in a campaign, curiosity-led or partially blind email can widen relevance and prevent a reader's preconceived objection from ending the conversation before the mechanism is understood.

Blind does not mean deceptive:

- Withhold only the detail needed to create a legitimate open loop.
- Make the email's promise and landing-page destination congruent.
- Satisfy the curiosity after the click.
- Become more explicit as the event, offer, or deadline gets closer.

Do not hide a material condition, disguise the commercial nature of an offer, or create a misleading click.

## Subject Lines Are the Gate

The subject line's job is to earn the open. If the email is not opened, the sales page, webinar, and offer cannot do their jobs.

- Build curiosity around a truthful, relevant idea.
- Use specificity, numbers, newness, contrast, or a meaningful open loop when supported.
- Number-led subjects can work because they promise concrete, scannable value.
- Early subjects may be less explicit; later subjects should match rising offer and deadline awareness.
- Do not overuse first-name personalization. A real friend rarely puts the recipient's name in every subject line.
- Study proven winners and the account's own performance rather than trying to make every subject unprecedented.
- Keep the subject, opening, body, CTA, and destination on the same conceptual scent.

Never use a subject line that the body or destination fails to fulfill.

## Build Belief With Truth

Nothing in the email should require invented hype.

- Follow a bold claim quickly with a demonstration, exact figure, screenshot, case study, example, or clear explanation.
- Exact numbers can increase believability when they are real. Never manufacture precision.
- Explain what the proof means for the reader instead of dropping evidence without interpretation.
- Give the real "reason why" behind an unusual claim, price, bonus, deadline, endorsement, or decision.

Keep these roles distinct:

- **Proof** demonstrates that a result or claim is real.
- **Social proof** shows visible group behavior or adoption.
- **Authority** adds credibility through a qualified expert, leader, or recognized source.

Use only proof, quotations, endorsements, and social response supplied or verified for the current promotion.

## Make the Benefit and Mechanism Clear

Connect the email's topic to the reader's core desired result. Do not stop at a tactic or feature when the reader cares about traffic, leads, customers, revenue, time, control, simplicity, or another practical outcome.

- Translate features into operational benefits and then into meaningful outcomes.
- Name or clarify the mechanism when it helps the reader understand why the result is possible.
- Surface preconceived objections and explain why the new mechanism changes the answer.
- Use "all the good without the bad" or old-way/new-way contrast only when the distinction is factual.
- Put the strongest or most consequential bullet last when a list benefits from a final "money bullet."

Newness is a strong amplifier when true. Words and ideas such as new, now, fresh, recently discovered, or not yet widely used must describe a real condition—not decorate an old claim.

## Use Story to Carry the Lesson

A quick backstory can earn attention and make the mechanism memorable. Use a real story to reveal the problem, discovery, change, or lesson; do not bolt on an anecdote merely because stories sell.

The story should move the campaign forward and connect naturally to the CTA. Never invent an event, emotion, quotation, or personal history for Mike.

## Format for Reading and Skimming

Make the email sound spoken and look easy to read:

- Use short paragraphs, substantial white space, and occasional one-sentence paragraphs.
- Most paragraphs should stay within roughly three to five sentences when the thought permits.
- Use fragments, questions, asides, and ellipses selectively to reproduce natural pacing.
- Use bullets to stop skimmers and organize genuine benefits, reasons, or examples.
- Use bold, italics, underlining, or capitals sparingly to direct the eye—not to shout continuously.
- Keep the presentation personal and credible rather than overly designed.

Do not imitate every historical surface habit. Modern readability and the current sending environment control typography.

## Match Length to the Relationship and Job

There is no universal rule that promotional email must be short.

- Use the length needed to complete the email's campaign job without repetition.
- Early in an unfamiliar relationship, shorter conversations may be more natural.
- Longer emails become more appropriate as rapport and interest grow.
- Avoid training the list to expect context-free "click here" messages that may win a single click while weakening the relationship.
- The immediate post-opt-in email is a useful exception: attention is high, so it may fulfill the promise, set expectations, establish the relationship, point to useful resources, and include a genuine surprise bonus.

## Calls to Action and Links

Make the next action clear and provide natural opportunities to take it.

- Place links where the reader has just been given a reason to click.
- Link relevant images when an image functions as part of the invitation.
- Multiple contextual CTAs are appropriate in a longer promotional email; do not scatter identical links without purpose.
- For webinar promotion, giving the day while asking the reader to click for the exact time can preserve the reason to visit the registration page.
- Prefer an absolute day such as "Wednesday" to relative language such as "tomorrow" when delayed opens or time zones could make the message confusing.
- Maintain congruence from email to landing page, thank-you page, event, and offer.

## Use the P.S. Deliberately

Many readers scan the P.S. even when they skim the body. Give it a real job:

- Restate the central premise or strongest reason to act.
- Answer a likely objection.
- Add a secondary incentive or verified bonus.
- Clarify replay, attendance, timing, or deadline conditions.
- Reinforce urgency as the campaign approaches a real close.

Change the P.S. as the campaign advances. Do not attach a generic P.S. to every email merely as a voice marker.

## Live Events, Replays, and Deadlines

Use truthful incentives to increase live attendance: access to slides, live-only material, a real prize, or content intentionally omitted from a replay. Keep the training or event's primary value central; a prize is normally secondary rather than the main subject-line promise.

- Do not announce a replay during a live event if doing so undermines attendance, but never misrepresent whether a replay will exist.
- Use deadline language only when the deadline is real and enforced.
- A time-sensitive note may tell late readers to disregard an expired message when that accurately reflects the offer.
- Avoid irreversible claims such as "never again" unless they are genuinely supportable.

## Editing Pass

After drafting:

1. Confirm the email has one clear campaign job.
2. Check that the subject earns attention without misleading.
3. Remove vendor-swipe language, corporate phrasing, clichés, fake intimacy, and unsupported excitement.
4. Strengthen the connection between mechanism, proof, benefit, and next action.
5. Verify every claim, number, testimonial, deadline, bonus, and attendance condition.
6. Tighten the visual rhythm for readers who skim.
7. Read the email aloud. It should sound like Mike speaking naturally to one person while deliberately moving that person toward an honest decision.

Wordsmithing can make the truth more memorable; it cannot make an unsupported statement true.
